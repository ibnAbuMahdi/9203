Question 1 (a)

AM Power Proof, Sideband Derivation & Frequency Spectrum

1. Proof of AM Total Power: \(P_t = P_c\left(1 + \frac{m^2}{2}\right)\)

A standard Double-Sideband Full-Carrier (DSB-FC) AM signal modulated by a single sinusoidal tone is expressed as:

$$s(t) = A_c \left[1 + m \cos(2\pi f_m t)\right] \cos(2\pi f_c t)$$

Expanding using trigonometric product identities \(\cos A \cos B = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(A+B) + \cos(A-B)]\):

$$s(t) = \underbrace{A_c \cos(2\pi f_c t)}_{\text{Carrier}} + \underbrace{\frac{m A_c}{2} \cos(2\pi(f_c + f_m)t)}_{\text{Upper Sideband (USB)}} + \underbrace{\frac{m A_c}{2} \cos(2\pi(f_c - f_m)t)}_{\text{Lower Sideband (LSB)}}$$

For any sinusoidal voltage waveform \(v(t) = V_p \cos(\omega t)\) dissipated across a load resistor \(R\), the average power is given by \(P = \frac{V_{rms}^2}{R} = \frac{V_p^2}{2R}\). Applying this to each spectral component:

$$\text{Carrier Power: } P_c = \frac{A_c^2}{2R}$$ $$\text{USB Power: } P_{USB} = \frac{(m A_c / 2)^2}{2R} = \frac{m^2 A_c^2}{8R} = \frac{m^2}{4} \left(\frac{A_c^2}{2R}\right) = \frac{m^2 P_c}{4}$$ $$\text{LSB Power: } P_{LSB} = \frac{(m A_c / 2)^2}{2R} = \frac{m^2 A_c^2}{8R} = \frac{m^2 P_c}{4}$$

The total transmitted power \(P_t\) is the sum of the powers of all independent frequency components:

$$P_t = P_c + P_{USB} + P_{LSB} = P_c + \frac{m^2 P_c}{4} + \frac{m^2 P_c}{4} = P_c + \frac{m^2 P_c}{2} = \boxed{P_c \left(1 + \frac{m^2}{2}\right)}$$

This completes the proof. \(\blacksquare\)

2. Mathematical Form of LSB and USB as a Function of \(P_c\) and Modulation Depth \(m\)

From the definition of carrier power \(P_c = \frac{A_c^2}{2R}\), the peak carrier amplitude is \(A_c = \sqrt{2 P_c R}\). Substituting this into the time-domain expressions:

$$\text{Peak amplitude of each sideband: } A_{SB} = \frac{m A_c}{2} = \frac{m \sqrt{2 P_c R}}{2} = m \sqrt{\frac{P_c R}{2}}$$

Hence, the instantaneous equations for the sidebands are:

$$s_{USB}(t) = m \sqrt{\frac{P_c R}{2}} \cos(2\pi (f_c + f_m)t)$$ $$s_{LSB}(t) = m \sqrt{\frac{P_c R}{2}} \cos(2\pi (f_c - f_m)t)$$

And their power representation in terms of \(P_c\) and \(m\) is:

$$P_{USB} = P_{LSB} = \frac{m^2 P_c}{4}$$

3. Frequency-Domain Representation of the AM Signal

Fig 1: Double-Sided/Positive Amplitude Spectrum of AM Signal

Spectral Characteristics:
  • Carrier component: Frequency \(f_c\), peak voltage \(A_c\) (or spectral line height \(A_c/2\)).
  • Upper Sideband (USB): Frequency \(f_c + f_m\), peak voltage \(\frac{m A_c}{2}\).
  • Lower Sideband (LSB): Frequency \(f_c - f_m\), peak voltage \(\frac{m A_c}{2}\).
  • Total Transmission Bandwidth: $$BW = (f_c + f_m) - (f_c - f_m) = 2 f_m$$
Question 1 (b)

Generation of SSB and DSB Signals (Filter & Phase Methods)

1. DSB Generation

Double Sideband Suppressed Carrier (DSB-SC) is generated by multiplying the message signal \(m(t)\) with the RF carrier \(c(t) = A_c \cos(2\pi f_c t)\) using a Balanced Modulator:

$$s_{\text{DSB-SC}}(t) = m(t) \cdot c(t) = A_m \cos(2\pi f_m t) \cdot A_c \cos(2\pi f_c t) = \frac{A_c A_m}{2} \cos(2\pi(f_c+f_m)t) + \frac{A_c A_m}{2} \cos(2\pi(f_c-f_m)t)$$

Method (i): Filter Method for SSB

Fig 2a: Filter Method Block Diagram

Operation:
  1. The message signal \(m(t)\) and carrier \(A_c\cos(2\pi f_c t)\) are applied to a Balanced Modulator to suppress the carrier and yield DSB-SC.
  2. The DSB-SC signal is passed through a high-Q Sideband Filter (Band-Pass Filter):
    • Passing frequencies above \(f_c\) yields USB: \(s(t) = \frac{A_c A_m}{2}\cos(2\pi(f_c+f_m)t)\).
    • Passing frequencies below \(f_c\) yields LSB: \(s(t) = \frac{A_c A_m}{2}\cos(2\pi(f_c-f_m)t)\).
Limitation: Requires extremely sharp filter roll-off, difficult to achieve at very high carrier frequencies without multi-stage frequency up-conversion.

Method (ii): Phase-Shift Method (Hartley Modulator)

Fig 2b: Phase-Shift (Hartley) Method Block Diagram

Operation:
  1. The baseband signal \(m(t) = A_m \cos(\omega_m t)\) is fed directly to Balanced Modulator 1, and through a \(90^\circ\) phase shifter (Hilbert transform) to produce \(\hat{m}(t) = A_m \sin(\omega_m t)\) into Balanced Modulator 2.
  2. The carrier oscillator output \(A_c\cos(\omega_c t)\) drives Modulator 1 directly and passes through a \(-90^\circ\) phase shifter to produce \(A_c\sin(\omega_c t)\) for Modulator 2.
  3. Outputs of the two modulators: $$v_1(t) = A_c A_m \cos(\omega_c t) \cos(\omega_m t) = \frac{A_c A_m}{2}[\cos(\omega_c+\omega_m)t + \cos(\omega_c-\omega_m)t]$$ $$v_2(t) = A_c A_m \sin(\omega_c t) \sin(\omega_m t) = \frac{A_c A_m}{2}[\cos(\omega_c-\omega_m)t - \cos(\omega_c+\omega_m)t]$$
  4. Summing / subtracting produces: $$\text{USB: } v_1(t) - v_2(t) = A_c A_m \cos(\omega_c + \omega_m)t$$ $$\text{LSB: } v_1(t) + v_2(t) = A_c A_m \cos(\omega_c - \omega_m)t$$

Question 3 (a) & Part ii (SSB)

AM Power Calculations, Current Variations & SSB PEP

Part 3 (a): Current \(I_c = 7\text{ A}\), Modulation \(m = 90\% = 0.9\), Antenna Impedance \(R = 50\,\Omega\)

1. Carrier Power (\(P_c\)): $$P_c = I_c^2 \cdot R = (7)^2 \times 50 = 49 \times 50 = \mathbf{2450\text{ W}}$$
2. Total Power (\(P_t\)): $$P_t = P_c\left(1 + \frac{m^2}{2}\right) = 2450 \left(1 + \frac{0.9^2}{2}\right) = 2450 (1 + 0.405) = 2450 \times 1.405 = \mathbf{3442.25\text{ W}}$$
3. Power in Each Sideband (\(P_{USB} = P_{LSB}\)): $$P_{USB} = P_{LSB} = \frac{m^2 P_c}{4} = \frac{(0.81) \times 2450}{4} = \frac{1984.5}{4} = \mathbf{496.125\text{ W}}$$
4. Powers in dB (referenced to 1 W): $$P_c(\text{dBW}) = 10 \log_{10}(2450) = 10 \times 3.38917 = \mathbf{33.89\text{ dBW}}$$ $$P_t(\text{dBW}) = 10 \log_{10}(3442.25) = 10 \times 3.53684 = \mathbf{35.37\text{ dBW}}$$ $$P_{SB}(\text{dBW}) = 10 \log_{10}(496.125) = 10 \times 2.69559 = \mathbf{26.96\text{ dBW}}$$
5. Powers in dBm (referenced to 1 mW = \(10^{-3}\text{ W}\)): $$\text{Formula: } P(\text{dBm}) = P(\text{dBW}) + 30\text{ dB}$$ $$P_c(\text{dBm}) = 33.89 + 30 = \mathbf{63.89\text{ dBm}}$$ $$P_t(\text{dBm}) = 35.37 + 30 = \mathbf{65.37\text{ dBm}}$$ $$P_{SB}(\text{dBm}) = 26.96 + 30 = \mathbf{56.96\text{ dBm}}$$

Transmitter Current Change: \(I_c = 7\text{ A}\) to \(I_t = 9\text{ A}\)

The relation between total antenna current \(I_t\) and unmodulated carrier current \(I_c\) is:

$$I_t = I_c \sqrt{1 + \frac{m^2}{2}}$$ $$\left(\frac{I_t}{I_c}\right)^2 = 1 + \frac{m^2}{2} \implies \left(\frac{9}{7}\right)^2 = 1 + \frac{m^2}{2}$$ $$\frac{81}{49} - 1 = \frac{m^2}{2} \implies \frac{32}{49} = \frac{m^2}{2} \implies m^2 = \frac{64}{49}$$ $$m = \sqrt{\frac{64}{49}} = \frac{8}{7} \approx \mathbf{1.1429}$$
✦ Modulation Coefficient: \(m = \frac{8}{7} \approx \mathbf{1.143}\)
✦ Percentage Modulation: \(\%m = 1.1429 \times 100\% = \mathbf{114.29\%}\)
Note: Because \(m > 1.0\), the transmitter is in an overmodulated condition, causing envelope distortion and adjacent channel interference (splatter).

Part (X2) ii: SSB Transmitter Peak Envelope Power (PEP) & Average Power

Given: Peak-to-peak voltage \(V_{pp} = 400\text{ V}\), Antenna load \(R = 75\,\Omega\).

1. Peak Voltage: $$V_{\text{peak}} = \frac{V_{pp}}{2} = \frac{400}{2} = 200\text{ V}$$
2. Peak Envelope Power (PEP):

PEP is the average power supplied during one radio frequency cycle at the crest of the modulation envelope:

$$\text{PEP} = \frac{V_{peak}^2}{2 R} = \frac{(200)^2}{2 \times 75} = \frac{40000}{150} = \mathbf{266.67\text{ W}}$$
3. Average Power (\(P_{avg}\)):

For a standard single continuous audio tone modulating an SSB transmitter, the envelope amplitude is constant over time, giving:

$$P_{avg} = \text{PEP} = \mathbf{266.67\text{ W}}$$ (Note: Under a standard two-tone test convention with two equal sine waves, \(P_{avg} = \frac{\text{PEP}}{2} = 133.33\text{ W}\). For single sinusoidal carrier: \(P_{avg} = 266.67\text{ W}\).)

Formatting Block with Different Information Sources

Fig 4: Detailed Formatting Block Handling Analog, Discrete and Digital Sources

Explanation of Formatting Operations:

  • Analog Sources (Voice, Video): Must undergo formatting via Pulse Code Modulation (PCM):
    1. Sampler: Samples the continuous-time signal at or above the Nyquist rate (\(f_s \ge 2 W\)).
    2. Quantizer: Maps continuous amplitude samples into \(L = 2^n\) discrete amplitude levels.
    3. Encoder: Converts quantized levels into an \(n\)-bit binary code word.
  • Textual / Discrete Sources (Keyboard characters): Already discrete in amplitude, directly mapped via ASCII/Unicode character encoding.
  • Binary Data Sources (Files): Require framing, parallel-to-serial conversion, or line coding.

ii) Three Prime Needs Motivating the Development/Evolution of DCS

  1. Regenerative Repeaters & Noise Immunity: Unlike analog systems where amplifier noise accumulates additively over distances, digital repeaters detect, re-time, and regenerate clean pulses, eliminating noise accumulation entirely.
  2. Error Detection and Correction Coding: Digital systems can introduce structured redundancy (Channel Coding like parity, Hamming, Reed-Solomon, LDPC) allowing receivers to automatically detect and correct transmission errors caused by noisy channels.
  3. Universal Integration & Cryptographic Security: All diverse media (voice, video, text, sensor telemetry) are formatted into uniform binary bitstreams, allowing unified multiplexing, routing, digital storage, and unbreakable mathematical encryption (AES, RSA).

Three Digital Modulation Techniques

Modulation AcronymFull MeaningOperating Principle
ASKAmplitude Shift KeyingCarrier amplitude switches between discrete levels to represent binary '1' and '0' (e.g. OOK: Carrier ON for '1', OFF for '0').
FSKFrequency Shift KeyingCarrier frequency shifts between discrete frequencies \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) corresponding to binary '1' and '0'.
PSK / QPSKPhase Shift Keying / Quadrature PSKCarrier phase shifts between discrete constellation points. In QPSK, 4 phases (\(45^\circ, 135^\circ, 225^\circ, 315^\circ\)) represent 2 bits per symbol.

Two Advantages and Disadvantages of an Analog Communication System (ACS)

Advantages of ACS Disadvantages of ACS
1. Circuit Simplicity & Low Cost: Transmitters and receivers do not require analog-to-digital converters, complex digital DSP chips, or synchronization clocks, making hardware straightforward and inexpensive. 1. Susceptibility to Noise & Distortion: Analog signals are continuous; any noise, distortion, or crosstalk injected in the channel adds directly to the signal and cannot be removed without damaging information.
2. Natural Audio Fidelity: Direct analog modulation (like wideband FM) does not suffer from quantization noise or digitization delay, providing natural continuous sound reproduction within channel bandwidth. 2. Lack of Error Correction & Encryption: Analog systems cannot implement mathematical channel error correction algorithms and are very difficult to encrypt securely compared to digital bitstreams.

Part 5 (a)(ii): Simplified Bessel's Expression and Total Power

For a single-tone angle modulated signal \(s(t) = A_c \cos(2\pi f_c t + \beta \sin(2\pi f_m t))\), applying the Jacobi–Anger expansion expresses the signal as an infinite summation of Bessel functions of the first kind:

$$\boxed{s(t) = A_c \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} J_n(\beta) \cos\bigl[2\pi(f_c + n f_m)t\bigr]}$$

where:

  • \(J_n(\beta)\) is the Bessel function of the first kind of order \(n\) with argument \(\beta\).
  • \(n = 0\) corresponds to the carrier component at \(f_c\) with amplitude \(A_c J_0(\beta)\).
  • \(n = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots\) represent pairs of symmetric sidebands at \(f_c \pm n f_m\) with amplitudes \(A_c J_n(\beta)\).

Total Power Required to Transmit this Signal

The average power dissipated into load resistance \(R\) by each individual spectral component is:

$$P_n = \frac{[A_c J_n(\beta)]^2}{2 R} = \frac{A_c^2}{2 R} J_n^2(\beta)$$

Summing the power across all infinite spectral components from \(n = -\infty\) to \(\infty\):

$$P_t = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} P_n = \frac{A_c^2}{2 R} \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} J_n^2(\beta)$$

By the fundamental Bessel identity (Parseval's identity for Bessel series), \(\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} J_n^2(\beta) = 1\) for any real value of \(\beta\). Therefore:

$$\boxed{P_t = \frac{A_c^2}{2 R} = P_c}$$
Key Principle: Frequency and phase modulation do not alter the signal amplitude. Total power in an angle-modulated wave is constant and entirely independent of modulation index \(\beta\). Modulation merely redistributes power between the carrier and sidebands.
Question 5 (b)

FM Signal Analysis: \(v_{FM}(t) = 2000 \cos(2\pi \times 10^8 t + 2 \sin(\pi \times 10^4 t))\text{ V}\), \(R = 75\,\Omega\)

Part 5 (b)(i): Extracting Signal Parameters

Standard sinusoidal FM equation:

$$v(t) = A_c \cos\bigl(2\pi f_c t + \beta \sin(2\pi f_m t)\bigr)$$

Comparing term by term with \(v_{FM}(t) = 2000 \cos\bigl(2\pi \cdot 10^8 t + 2 \sin(\pi \cdot 10^4 t)\bigr)\):

1. Carrier Peak Amplitude: $$A_c = 2000\text{ V}$$ 2. Carrier Frequency (\(f_c\)): $$2\pi f_c = 2\pi \times 10^8 \implies \mathbf{f_c = 10^8\text{ Hz} = 100\text{ MHz}}$$ 3. Modulating Frequency (\(f_m\)): $$2\pi f_m = \pi \times 10^4 \implies \mathbf{f_m = \frac{10^4}{2} = 5000\text{ Hz} = 5\text{ kHz}}$$
4. Modulation Index (\(\beta\)): $$\mathbf{\beta = 2}$$ 5. Frequency Deviation (\(\Delta f\)): $$\Delta f = \beta f_m = 2 \times 5\text{ kHz} = \mathbf{10\text{ kHz}}$$ 6. Percentage Modulation:

Using maximum standard broadcast FM deviation \(\Delta f_{max} = 75\text{ kHz}\):

$$\% \text{Modulation} = \frac{\Delta f}{\Delta f_{max}} \times 100\% = \frac{10}{75} \times 100\% = \mathbf{13.33\%}$$
7. Transmitted Power (\(P_t\)): $$P_t = \frac{A_c^2}{2 R} = \frac{(2000)^2}{2 \times 75} = \frac{4 \times 10^6}{150} = \frac{40000}{1.5} = \mathbf{26{,}666.67\text{ W} \approx 26.67\text{ kW}}$$

$$BW_{\text{Carson}} = 2(\Delta f + f_m) = 2(10\text{ kHz} + 5\text{ kHz}) = 2(15\text{ kHz}) = \mathbf{30\text{ kHz}}$$

Carson's rule captures ~98% of the total transmitted signal energy.
